How much of the ocean floor has actually been measured?

Seafloor Atlas is a free interactive 3D globe of the whole ocean floor, built from the GEBCO 2026 grid. It shows the shape of the seafloor and, for every place, whether its depth was measured by a ship or only estimated from space.

What you can do on the globe

  • See what is measured and what is only estimated, anywhere on Earth, down to 460 m detail.
  • Draw a depth profile across a trench or ridge, for example across Challenger Deep (almost 11 km).
  • Lower or raise the sea: at −120 m, the level of the last ice age, Sundaland, Sahul and Beringia become land.
  • Look at the water: temperature, salinity and density from the surface to 5 km, month by month.
  • Play time: earthquakes since 1900, internet cables, offshore wind farms and seafloor mapping 2019 → 2026.
  • Follow winds, currents and phytoplankton through the seasons, and see marine life records and their gaps.
  • Human activity at sea: submarine cables, shipping lanes, shipwrecks, oil and gas platforms, pipelines, wind farms.

Frequently asked questions

How much of the ocean floor has been mapped?

In the GEBCO 2026 grid, 30.8 % of the seafloor area is coded as directly measured by ships and other surveys (multibeam and single-beam sonar, lidar, seismic). The official Seabed 2030 figure for the same grid is 28.7 %; it uses a stricter, depth-dependent resolution rule. The rest of the seafloor is estimated from satellite gravity or interpolated between measurements.

Why is so little of the seafloor measured?

The ocean covers 361.7 million km² (70.9 % of Earth). A survey ship measures only a strip a few kilometres wide under its track, and most of the deep ocean is far from shipping lanes and coasts. The least-measured places are the abyssal plains (26.7 % at 4–5 km depth), the southern mid-latitudes (19–20 % at 40–50° S) and the high seas (26.4 %, against 38.0 % inside national waters).

How fast is the measured share growing?

Between GEBCO 2025 and GEBCO 2026 it went from 29.7 % → 30.8 %, +4.1 million km² in one year on the same ocean mask. Part of every release is also re-coded between "measured" and "estimated", which this atlas tracks release by release.

What is the deepest point of the ocean?

Challenger Deep, at the southern end of the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific, almost 11 km below sea level. At the 14 deepest places the GEBCO 2026 grid lies within a median of 10 m of published surveys.

How much water is in the ocean?

On the GEBCO 2026 grid the ocean holds 1.338 billion km³ with a mean depth of 3,702 m, within 0.5 % of an earlier published estimate (Charette and Smith 2010).

Where does the data come from?

Depth and the measured/estimated coding come from the GEBCO Compilation Group grids (2019–2026) and their Type Identifier grids. Other layers use World Ocean Atlas 2023 (temperature, salinity), NASA MODIS-Aqua (phytoplankton), NCEP/NCAR and OSCAR (winds, currents), USGS ComCat (earthquakes), OBIS and AquaMaps (marine life), Marine Regions (national waters), seamount catalogues, OpenStreetMap, Wikidata, the World Bank and TeleGeography. Each layer keeps its source licence.

Can I use the data and the code?

Yes. The code is MIT-licensed. Each data file keeps the licence of its source, listed per file in LICENSES.md; some layers are non-commercial or ShareAlike. The depths are not for navigation.

No. It is an independent open-source project built on their public data and is not endorsed by them.

For researchers

The measured share is computed from the public GEBCO Type Identifier grids of every release from 2019 to 2026 (codes 10–17 = direct measurement), on one fixed ocean mask, with every number traceable to a script. Code, derived data and figures are archived on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.24254651, version 1.1.1; all versions doi:10.5281/zenodo.24228190). A preprint describing the method has been submitted to EarthArXiv.
